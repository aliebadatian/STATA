    STATA Tutorial: Event Studies – Google Stock (Session 5)

    📌 Note:
     All the full STATA codes used in this tutorial, together with training exercises and questions, are available for download on your Moodle page. Be sure to retrieve them so you can follow along and practice.

    Introduction

    Hey everyone! 👋 In this session, we’re going to play the role of market detectives. We’re going to see how the stock market reacted to Google’s big announcement on August 10, 2015—when Google reorganized under Alphabet.

    Event studies are a neat tool in finance and econometrics. The idea is simple: compare what actually happened (the stock’s return) with what we expected to happen if nothing special occurred. The difference? That’s called the abnormal return (AR), and it tells us how the market responded to the event.

    We’ll go through three ways to measure abnormal returns, visualize them, and focus on the day of the announcement. Let's dive in!

    💡 Tip: Think of abnormal returns as the “surprise” in the stock price caused by the event.

    1. Getting Started

    First, let’s clean our workspace and set the folder where our files live:

    clear all
cd "C:\Users\ebada\Desktop\STATA S5"  // replace with your path

    It’s always a good idea to log your session so you can revisit your work later:

    log using "log_stata_session5.log", replace

    2. Loading the Dataset

    We’re using google_stock.dta, which has daily returns for Google and the S\&P 500 from May to August 2015.

    causaldata google_stock.dta, use clear
describe

    Take a look at the dataset. Notice the daily returns for Google (google_return) and the market (sp500_return). These are the building blocks for our abnormal return calculations.

    3. Time Series Setup

    Because this is daily data, we need to tell Stata it’s a time series:

    tsset date

    Now Stata knows we’re dealing with time-ordered data. This lets us plot returns over time easily.

    4. Defining Estimation and Event Periods

    Before calculating abnormal returns, we need two periods:

    1. Estimation period: This is our “normal” period before the event. We use it to figure out what Google’s returns normally look like.
    2. Observation period: This is around the event, where we measure abnormal returns.
    gen est_period = date < td(01aug2015)
gen obs_period = inrange(date, td(01aug2015), td(31aug2015))

    Think of it as: we’re learning Google’s “usual behavior” first, then checking how it reacts to the surprise.

    5. Abnormal Returns – Mean Method

    The simplest way to get abnormal returns is to compare daily returns to the pre-event average:

    summarize google_return if est_period
gen mean_google = r(mean)

gen AR_mean = google_return - mean_google
label var AR_mean "Abnormal Return (Mean method)"

    So here, we’re just saying: “What’s different today from the usual Google return?”

    6. Abnormal Returns – Market Method

    Next, we account for general market movements. If the whole market is up or down, we don’t want to confuse that with Google’s reaction.

    gen AR_market = google_return - sp500_return
label var AR_market "Abnormal Return (Market method)"

    Now the abnormal return is the difference between Google’s return and the S\&P 500 return.

    7. Abnormal Returns – Risk-Adjusted Method

    The risk-adjusted method is a bit fancier. We model Google’s return based on market movements (regression) and then calculate the surprise:

    regress google_return sp500_return if est_period
predict expected_ret, xb

gen AR_risk = google_return - expected_ret
label var AR_risk "Abnormal Return (Risk-adjusted method)"

    Here, we’re asking: “Given the market’s move today, how much did Google deviate from expectation?”

    8. Visualizing Abnormal Returns

    Graphs are your friends—they make patterns obvious. Let’s start with risk-adjusted abnormal returns for August 2015:

    tsline AR_risk if obs_period, tline(10aug2015) ///
    title("Abnormal Return – Google (August 2015)") ///
    ytitle("Abnormal Return") ///
    yscale(range(-.06 .06)) ylabel(-.06(.02).06, angle(0) format(%4.2f)) ///
    xtitle("Date")

graph export "AR_risk_Google_August2015.png", replace

    Now let’s compare all three methods in one plot:

    tsline AR_mean AR_market AR_risk if obs_period, tline(10aug2015) ///
    title("Abnormal Returns (Different Methods)") ///
    legend(order(1 "Mean" 2 "Market" 3 "Risk-adjusted")) ///
    ytitle("Abnormal Return") ///
    yscale(range(-.06 .06)) ylabel(-.06(.02).06, angle(0) format(%4.2f)) ///
    xtitle("Date")

graph export "AR_comparison_Google_August2015.png", replace

    See how the abnormal returns spike on August 10? That’s the market reacting to the announcement.

    9. Inspecting the Event Day

    Curious about the exact numbers on the announcement day?

    list date google_return AR_mean AR_market AR_risk if date==td(10aug2015), noobs

    Now you can see the actual return and how “abnormal” it was according to each method.

    10. Interpreting Abnormal Returns

    Alright, now that we have our abnormal returns and graphs, let’s talk about what they actually mean. Remember: abnormal returns are the difference between what happened and what we expected.

    Positive AR: The stock did better than expected. For Google on August 10, a positive spike would mean the market reacted favorably to the announcement.

    Negative AR: The stock did worse than expected. A drop tells us investors were surprised negatively or worried about the news.

    Compare the three methods:

    Mean method: Simple, just compares against the average historical return. Quick and intuitive, but doesn’t account for market-wide movements.

    Market method: Adjusts for the overall market movement. This helps isolate Google-specific effects from general market swings.

    Risk-adjusted method: The most sophisticated of the three. It accounts for Google’s typical sensitivity to the market. If Google usually moves with the market, this method tells us whether today’s move was unusual even given the market’s movement.

    💡 Tip: Look at the spike on August 10 in your graphs. A clear positive or negative peak indicates that the announcement had a meaningful impact. The bigger the spike, the stronger the market reaction.

    Also, check the magnitude of the abnormal returns:

    Small AR (~0.5%) may indicate a mild reaction.

    Large AR (>2–3%) usually signals a major market reaction.

    Finally, compare across methods. If all three methods show a similar spike, that’s strong evidence the event mattered. If they differ, consider market conditions or estimation assumptions—sometimes, choosing the right benchmark makes a difference.
