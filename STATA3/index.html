    STATA Tutorial – Session 3: Data Exploration and Regression Practice

    📌 Note:
     All the full STATA codes used in this tutorial, together with training exercises and questions, are available for download on your Moodle page. Be sure to retrieve them so you can follow along and practice.

    Introduction & Motivation

    In this session, we continue building your skills in exploratory data analysis (EDA) and regression modeling. This exercises push you beyond simple summary statistics: you will create new variables, explore relationships with tabulations and regressions, test for nonlinear patterns, and interpret results in context. The goal is that by the end, you feel comfortable exploring real datasets, diagnosing patterns, and modeling relationships with control variables and interactions.

    We’ll work with two datasets in two parts:

    • Part 1: hsbdemo.dta — data on high school students (grades, school types, programs, socioeconomic status)
    • Part 2: restaurant_inspections.dta — data on restaurant health inspection scores and number of locations

    As usual, we will walk step by step, explaining why we run each command, what the output means, and what to watch out for.

    Part 1 — Exploring Student Data & Regressions

    1. Load the Dataset and Inspect Variables

    use "hsbdemo.dta", clear describe codebook female ses schtyp prog
    • use ... , clear loads the dataset and clears any existing memory.
    • describe gives you variable names, types, labels, and counts.
    • codebook (for selected variables) shows you unique values, missing counts, and distributions.

    At this point, you should check:
    - What coding scheme is used (e.g. is female = 0/1? What values does schtyp take?). be careful and check if the variable have numeric values only or numeric and labels). - Which values of prog (program) exist — e.g. academic, general, vocational and the numeric value of each one.

    2. Create an Overall “Score” Variable

    We want a new variable score that is the average of five subject grades: read, write, math, science, socst. 

    gen score = (read + write + math + science + socst) / 5 
label variable score "mean score"
    • gen score = … creates the new variable.
    • We divide by 5 because there are five subjects.
    • label variable gives a human-readable label for output display.

    Another, more robust way to write it by using rowmean:

    gen score = rowmean(read + write + math + science + socst)
label variable score "mean score"

    Why do this?
    Because having a single composite score simplifies analysis (fewer regressions) and gives you a summary measure of each student’s performance.

    3. Compute Averages by Gender (and Overall)

    We’d like to know how male vs. female students perform, and also the overall average.

    *Overall summary
summarize score

*Summary by gender
bysort female: summarize score

*another way to get the summary by gender
summarize score if female == 0
summarize score if female == 1
    • bysort female: summarize score runs the summary (mean, sd, min, max) separately for female = 0 and female = 1.
    • The summarize … if … lines isolate each group explicitly.
    • A final summarize gives you the overall average.

    From the output, note the mean score for males, for females, and overall. Are there noticeable differences?

    ⚠️ Make sure to check the number of observations in each group — differences might be due to sample sizes or missing data.

    4. Tabulate Program by School Type

    We want two types of information:

    1. Within private schools, what percentage of students attend an academic program?
    2. Among students in academic programs, what share come from private schools?
    tab schtyp prog, row 
tab schtyp prog, col
    • tab … , row gives row percentages (i.e. within each schtyp, the distribution of prog).
    • tab … , col gives column percentages (i.e. among each prog category, the distribution of schtyp).

    Interpret carefully:

    • From the row table, read across a school type to see what proportion of its students are in each program.
    • From the col table, read down a program to see how many come from each school type.

    5. Percentage of Students Enrolled in English (within Public Schools)

    The question is: within public schools, what fraction of students are enrolled in an English/honors program?

    tab schtyp honors, row

    Here, reading the row for schtyp = public gives you the proportion enrolled in honors within public schools.

    6. Regression: Program Type → Mean Score

    We now ask: does the type of educational program a student attends predict their average score?

    reg score i.prog
    • i.prog tells STATA to treat prog as a categorical (factor) variable, creating dummy variables for each program (omitting one as baseline again check the codebook to see which category is listed as the first).
    • The regression output gives coefficients for each non-baseline program: how much higher (or lower) their average score is, relative to the baseline, holding nothing else constant.

    Check:
    - Which program is the omitted (baseline) category?
    - Are coefficient estimates statistically significant?
    - What does the constant _cons represent (the expected score in the omitted program)?

    Now let's interpret the results together:

    - We have the baseline here is the general category, both of academic and vocation are relative to general
    - Students who are in academic program score on average ~5.10 points higher, while for students who are in vocational program average score ~4.50 points lower. Both of the coefficients are statistically significant at the 1% level (p<0.01).
    - Intercept: The mean score for students who are enrolled in general program is 50.83
    - R-squared: 24.6% of variation in mean scores explained by the program type alone.

    7. Regression with Controls: SES & School Type

    We expand the model, adding socioeconomic status (ses) and school type (schtyp) to control for background differences:

    reg score i.prog i.ses i.schtyp

    With control variables, you can see: - Whether the gaps across prog categories shrink or persist after controlling for SES and school type. - The estimated effect of SES categories and being in a private vs public school on the mean score (relative to omitted reference groups).

    Interpretation steps:

    1. Look at each i.prog coefficient: is it still large after controls?
    2. Look at i.ses and i.schtyp: which levels are significant, and in which direction?
    3. Does the model’s R² increase substantially (i.e. do controls explain more of the variation)?

    let's interpret it together: We adding the socioeconomic and school type variables to the above model:

    • The coefficient for the academic slightly deceases to ~4.08 but still positive and statistically significant, same for vocational, the coefficient remains negative ~-4.81 and statistically significant. This conclude that students who are enrolled in academic programs outperform those in general programs and students in general programs outperform those who are in vocational program

    • Students from middle and high SES outperform students coming from low SES by 3.4 and 5.7 score, respectively. This highlight the strong positive influence of socioeconomic status on academic performance.

    • The type of school doesn’t effect the score as the coefficient of private school is small and not significant (p = .734)

    • Looking at the R-squared, the model explained ~31% of the score variation, which represents a moderate improvement in the explanatory compared to the simpler model.

    8. Predicted Mean Scores for Specific Profiles

    We want to compute predicted average scores for students in different programs, conditional on being in a private school and having high SES.

    margins prog, at(schtyp = 2 ses = 3)
    • at(...) fixes schtyp and ses at specific values.
    • This gives you predicted means of score by prog, holding school type and SES constant.

    Interpretation:

    • Suppose schtyp=2 corresponds to private school, and ses=3 to high SES. Then you’re comparing predicted scores across programs within those conditions.
    • You can compare which program yields the highest expected score for that profile.

    Part 2 — Restaurant Inspections & Nonlinear Models

    1. Load Restaurant Data

    use "restaurant_inspections.dta", clear

    Make sure your working directory is correct so STATA finds the file.

    2. Reproduce Table 13.2 Regressions

    We run two regressions on health inspection scores (inspection_score) and number of locations (numberoflocations):

    reg inspection_score numberoflocations 
estimates store m1  

reg inspection_score numberoflocations year 
estimates store m2
    • The first regression is the bivariate relationship.
    • The second adds a control for year to see whether time effects change the estimate.

    3. Export Regression Table

    esttab m1 m2 using "regression_table.doc", se r2 ar2 replace
    • esttab (from estout package) neatly formats regression results into a Word .doc file.
    • You include standard errors (se), R² (r2), and adjusted R² (ar2).

    If not installed yet, run:

    ssc install estout
    • You can always ask a LLM model (for example, chatgpt) to help you create the table showing the results of the two regression.

      Example Prompt:
      Act as a Stata specialist.
      I have already run these two models:
      reg inspection_score numberoflocations
      reg inspection_score numberoflocations year
      Please provide the Stata command to combine the results from both of the above regressions into one table (side by side) showing coefficients, robust standard errors, R-squared, N, and significance stars. Then, I want to save that table in my current working directory as RTF (for Word).

    4. Check for Nonlinearity in Number of Locations

    We suspect the relationship may not be purely linear. Let's allow for a quadratic term:

    gen numloc2 = numberoflocations^2 
reg inspection_score numberoflocations numloc2

    Alternatively, more elegantly:

    reg inspection_score c.numberoflocations##c.numberoflocations
    • c.var##c.var creates both the linear and squared term automatically.
    • Check whether the coefficient on the squared term is significant, and examine the sign (positive or negative curvature).

    • If the squared term is significant and negative, the relationship may be concave (diminishing returns).

    • Read lecture #5 and chapter 13.2 for more context.

    5. Marginal Effects of Locations

    We want to know how marginal changes in number of locations affect inspection scores:

    margins, dydx(numberoflocations) 
margins, dydx(numberoflocations) at(numberoflocations = 100)
margins, dydx(numberoflocations) at(numberoflocations = 750)
    • The first margins gives an average marginal effect.
    • The others compute marginal effects at specific values (e.g. for small or large chains).
    • You can plot these with marginsplot to visualize how the effect evolves.

    6. Interaction with Weekend Inspections

    We might think that inspections done on weekends behave differently. To test this:

    reg inspection_score c.numberoflocations##i.weekend year, robust 
margins, dydx(numberoflocations) at(weekend = 1) 
margins, dydx(numberoflocations) at(weekend = 0)
    • c.var##i.var lets you interact a continuous variable with a categorical one (here, weekend).
    • robust gives robust standard errors (helpful with heteroskedasticity).
    • margins, dydx(...) at(...) gives how the slope (effect of number of locations) differs on weekends vs non-weekends.

    Interpretation:

    • Does the number-of-locations slope differ on weekends?
    • Which group has steeper slope — weekend or non-weekend?
    • Are differences statistically meaningful?

    If you look at your regression table you can find:

    • On weekends

      • Each additional restaurant location is associated with a negative effect on the inspection score by -0.029 points. And the effect is statistically significant (p = 0.008). The confidence interval (–0.05 to –0.0075) means we’re 95% confident the true effect lies within that range is always negative.

    • On weekdays

      • Each additional location decreases inspection scores by about 0.019 points, this effect is highly significant (p < 0.001) and very precisely estimated (small standard error).

    • Comparison (weekend vs. weekday)

      • The effect of having more locations is slightly more negative on weekends (–0.029 vs –0.019), but not dramatically so. Since the interaction term in the regression numberoflocations#weekend wasn’t statistically significant (p = 0.369), we can conclude that this small difference is not statistically meaningful.

      • This means: The negative relationship between the number of locations and inspection score is essentially the same on weekends and weekdays.

    Conceptual Takeaways & Guidance

    • Descriptive statistics and tabulations (summaries, tab) help you see patterns, spot anomalies, and form hypotheses.
    • Regression formalizes a relationship by estimating a “line of best fit” while controlling for other factors.
    • Nonlinear models (quadratic, interactions) allow more flexibility: you are not locked into straight-line assumptions.
    • Marginal effects and margins are your friend — they translate coefficient estimates into more interpretable quantities (how much a small change in X affects Y).
    • Always inspect, think, question — e.g. look at sample sizes, variable coding, potential outliers, and whether causality can be claimed.
